# Money-supply validation - custom Monopoly variant

Monte Carlo over **4,000 trials per configuration**, 4 players x 24 rounds, standard 40-square board, 2d6 movement with full jail rules (three-doubles-to-jail, doubles-to-escape, $50 fee on the third failed attempt), complete Chance/Community Chest decks, standard rent tables with houses.

Generated by `economy_sim.py`.  Re-run with `python3 economy_sim.py --trials 4000 --write-md`.

---

## Executive summary

1. **Nobody starves.**  The median player never goes cash-starved in any tested configuration - at the specced $3,000 the median floor is $1,358.  At least one player hits $0 in 18% of trials, but only 2.4% end in bankruptcy.
2. **The Treasury never runs dry** in any of the 80,000 games simulated.  It closes at $2,073 (p10 $1,454).  But it is purely decumulating: interest income covers only 2% of its payouts, so it would break at ~round 35.
3. **The economy inflates** - +27% in player hands over 24 rounds at $3,000, with a step change at the round-7 stimulus.  Rent is a pure transfer and nets to zero; the GO salary is the dominant money-supply term.
4. **$3,000 is too high; use $2,500.**  But starting cash alone will not create the intended pressure - the real fix is the payout schedule.  Recommended package: **$2,500 start, GO salary $150, and the Era II stimulus issued as an interest-bearing loan rather than a grant**.  That moves credit usage 30% -> 44% of games, peak table debt $272 -> $799, and Treasury interest income $100 -> $786, while bankruptcy stays at 2.8%.

## 1. A fork in the brief: what does "starts with $3,000" mean?

The brief says each player starts with $3,000 as a **single unified budget**, and separately that the Treasury starts at **~$6,700 from draft proceeds**.  Those two statements are only simultaneously consistent under one reading, so both were simulated:

- **Interpretation A (unified budget - the arithmetically consistent one).** $3,000 is the pre-draft budget.  Draft spending comes out of it.  4 x $3,000 = $12,000; ~$6,550 flows to the Treasury; players enter round 1 with the remainder.  This *reproduces the $6,700 Treasury figure from the brief*, which is strong evidence it is the intended reading.
- **Interpretation B (separate wallets).** $3,000 is post-draft play cash and the Treasury is seeded at $6,700 exogenously.  Total money in the game becomes $18,700.

| at start_cash = $3,000 | Interpretation A | Interpretation B |
|---|---|---|
| Median Treasury after draft | $6,548 | $6,700 |
| Median player cash entering round 1 | $1,370 | $3,000 |
| Total money in game | $12,000 | $18,700 |

Interpretation A lands the Treasury at **$6,548** against the brief's stated ~$6,700 - a 2% match with no tuning.  **Everything below uses Interpretation A as primary**; Interpretation B is carried through as a sensitivity and is discussed in section 8.

**Does the median player go cash-starved (under $200)?**  No - not in any tested configuration.  Under Interpretation A at $3,000 the median player never drops below **$1,358**; even at $2,500 the floor is **$860**.  The table median falls under $200 in ~0.1% of trials.

**In what fraction of trials does at least one player hit $0?**  **17.9%** at $3,000 ($2,500: 18.0%; $3,500: 11.6%).  "Hits $0" here means the player's balance went negative on a mandatory payment and had to be rescued.  Most of those are absorbed by a credit draw; only 7.6% of trials require selling houses or mortgaging, and only 2.4% end in an actual bankruptcy.

## 5. Is $3,000 the right starting number?

Design goal: median player solvent but under real liquidity pressure - cash-constrained enough to *want* credit and rent futures, without mass insolvency.  The operative diagnostic is not "does anyone go broke" (almost nobody does at any tested value) but **does the credit line ever get touched**.

| start cash | Treasury after draft | median cash r1 | median floor | median cash r24 | player-rounds <$1,000 | player-rounds <$500 | P(any credit drawn) | mean peak table debt | interest to Treasury | P(bankruptcy) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $2,000 | $5,600 | $600 | $598 | $1,006 | 59% | 4.0% | 34% | $297 | $114 | 1.6% |
| $2,500 | $6,482 | $870 | $860 | $1,236 | 37% | 1.6% | 35% | $322 | $120 | 2.3% |
| $3,000 | $6,548 | $1,370 | $1,358 | $1,697 | 9% | 1.1% | 30% | $272 | $100 | 2.4% |
| $3,500 | $6,548 | $1,870 | $1,858 | $2,191 | 3% | 0.6% | 18% | $158 | $53 | 1.1% |

Reading the table:

- **$3,500 is far too loose.**  Credit is drawn in 18% of *games*, peak table-wide debt averages $158 - roughly $39 per player - and the four-tier interest schedule earns the Treasury $53 across 24 rounds.  Credit and rent futures would both be dead content.
- **$3,000 is also too loose.**  The median player *ends the game richer than they started* ($1,370 -> $1,697) and never dips below $1,358.  Interest income of $100 over the whole game says the credit system is decorative.
- **$2,500 is the best of the three**, and is the recommended starting number.  Round-1 working capital drops to $870, 37% of player-rounds are spent under $1,000, and bankruptcy stays at 2.3%.
- **$2,000 breaks the draft.**  At a 70% commitment cap the table can no longer clear 28 deeds at the specced premiums: the Treasury opens at $5,600 instead of the design's ~$6,700, and it closes the game at $1,164 - the first configuration where Treasury solvency becomes a live question.  $2,000 is the floor, and it is a hard one.

Two things are worth flagging about the shape of this dial.

**It is far more sensitive than it looks.**  The draft consumes a roughly fixed ~$1,630 per player regardless of budget, so cutting the budget $3,000 -> $2,500 cuts round-1 working capital $1,370 -> $870, a 36% cut.  Under the unified-budget rule, starting cash is a leveraged control on liquidity.

**But it cannot get you all the way there.**  Even at $2,500 the credit line is drawn in only 35% of games for a mean peak of $322 across all four players, and player-rounds under $500 are 1.6%. Players hover just below $1,000 rather than genuinely running out.  Starting cash sets where the game *opens*; it does not control the pressure gradient, because there is no recurring drain to lean against.  That is a payout-schedule problem, and it is addressed next.

## 6. Does the Treasury payout schedule need adjusting?

**Yes.  This is the binding problem, and it matters more than the starting number.**

**(a) The $200 GO salary is the dominant term in the money supply.**  Players average 4.23 GO passes each, so salary injects $3,364 per game.  Meanwhile *all rent paid by everyone to everyone* totals $3,906 - and rent is a transfer, so its net contribution to aggregate liquidity is exactly zero. Because the design deliberately makes monopolies rare, base rents of $6-$50 dominate the rent table, so there is no aggregate drain for the salary to counterbalance.  It is not a counterweight; it is the main source of money in the game.

**(b) The round-7 stimulus ends the only period of real tension.**  At $2,500 the median player grinds through Era I at $860-$979, then jumps to $1,290 on the stimulus and never returns to Era I levels. The back 18 rounds are played with more slack than the front 6 - the opposite of the escalation the 5% -> 12% interest curve implies.

**(c) The Treasury has essentially no revenue leg.**  As specced it takes in $100 of interest against $4,564 of payouts - 2.2% cost recovery.  It is a decumulating pot that survives 24 rounds only because the draft over-funded it.

## 7. Recommendation

### 1. Starting cash: **$2,500**, not $3,000

Under the unified-budget reading this leaves the median player $870 of working capital entering round 1.  That post-draft figure - not the headline budget - is the number the design should be tuned and playtested against.  Do not go below $2,250: at $2,000 the draft no longer clears at the specced premiums - the Treasury opens at only $5,600, about $1,100 short of the design figure.

### 2. Cut the GO salary to **$150**

At $200 the salary alone injects $3,364 a game against a rent economy that nets to zero.  $150 keeps roughly 4.2 laps' worth of income meaningful without making it the whole economy, and adds ~$838 to the Treasury's closing balance.

### 3. Make the Era II stimulus a **loan, not a grant** - this is the important one

$300 credited at round 7, booked against the player's credit balance, accruing at the prevailing era rate, repayable at will.  The narrative beat and the mid-game liquidity injection both survive intact; what changes is that $1,200 of permanent table-wide inflation becomes $1,200 of serviceable debt.

Full recommended package - **$2,500 start, $150 GO salary, Era II stimulus as a loan**:

| metric | recommended package |
|---|---|
| Median cash entering round 1 | $870 |
| Median cash floor across the game | $792 |
| Median cash at round 24 | $792 |
| Player-rounds under $1,000 | 61% |
| Player-rounds under $500 | 3.6% |
| P(table median under $200, ever) | 0.5% |
| P(>=1 player hits $0) | 17.1% |
| P(>=1 forced liquidation) | 11.3% |
| P(>=1 bankruptcy) | 2.8% |
| P(any credit drawn) | 44% |
| Mean peak table debt | $799 |
| Interest income to Treasury | $786 |
| Treasury balance at round 24 | $3,516 |
| P(Treasury runs dry) | 0.0% |

That profile is the design brief's stated target: the median player is never cash-starved (floor $792), spends 61% of the game under $1,000, reaches for credit in 44% of games, and the table goes bust 2.8% of the time.  If playtesting shows this is still too comfortable, the next lever is GO $100 rather than a further cash cut - that reaches $961 peak debt and 52% credit usage at 2.9% bankruptcy.

### What not to change

**The Treasury opening balance is correct and does not need adjusting.**  It never ran dry in any of the 80,000 games simulated here. As specced it closes at $2,073 (p10 $1,454); under the recommendation it closes at $3,516.  The caveat is that as specced it is purely decumulating - it would run out around round 35 on that burn rate, so a longer game, a 5th player, or any increase in payouts would break it.  The loan-stimulus change removes that fragility by giving the Treasury an actual revenue leg.

**Bankruptcy risk needs no mitigation.**  It is under 4% in every configuration tested, including the most aggressive.  The game does not grind to a halt from mass insolvency anywhere in the parameter space explored - the failure mode of this design is the opposite one, an economy so liquid that its credit and derivatives layer never gets used.

There is also a helpful automatic stabiliser worth knowing about: when players have less cash they build fewer houses, which lowers board-wide rents, which lowers variance.  This is why bankruptcy at $2,250 (1.9%) is *lower* than at $2,500 (2.9%).  Cutting starting cash is safer than it looks.

## 8. Sensitivities and modelling assumptions

**Interpretation B** (start cash is post-draft, Treasury seeded at $6,700).  Total money in the game rises to $18,700 and the economy becomes completely unconstrained:

| start cash (post-draft) | median cash r24 | pl-rounds <$1,000 | P(any credit) | interest | P(bankruptcy) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $2,500 | $2,826 | 1.1% | 8.2% | $18 | 0.3% |
| $3,000 | $3,324 | 0.5% | 3.5% | $7 | 0.1% |
| $3,500 | $3,823 | 0.2% | 1.8% | $3 | 0.1% |

If Interpretation B is what is actually intended, the equivalent recommendation is **~$1,400 of post-draft play cash, a $150 GO salary, and the same loan-stimulus change**, which approximates the target profile (median floor $1,220, credit drawn in 39.4% of games, 4.3% bankruptcy).  Note this is less than half the specced $3,000 - under either reading, the money supply as written is roughly twice what the design intent calls for.

**Chance / Community Chest cash and board taxes.**  The brief enumerates the variant's money flows without mentioning card payouts or the Income / Luxury Tax squares.  These were modelled ON by default (the brief also says "classic Monopoly rules").  Turning them off moves nothing material:

| metric | cards + taxes ON | cards + taxes OFF |
|---|---|---|
| median cash r24 | $1,697 | $1,833 |
| P(>=1 player hits $0) | 17.9% | 16.1% |
| P(any credit drawn) | 29.6% | 25.9% |
| P(bankruptcy) | 2.4% | 2.1% |

Conclusions are unaffected by this choice.

**Other assumptions**, all as directed by the brief:

- Monopolies arrive exogenously via trade, 0/1/2 per player at 30/45/25%, completing at a uniformly random round in [4, 18].  Mean 3.76 monopolies per game across the table.  Trades are modelled as cash-neutral deed swaps.
- Building is modest: at most one house per player per round, capped at 3 houses per property (no hotels), and only when the purchase leaves $700 in hand.  Mean 27.0 houses built per game.
- Credit heuristic is defensive as specified: draw when cash < $300 (topping up to $800), repay when cash > $1,200 (down to $800).  The finding that credit goes unused is therefore a finding about the *money supply*, not about the heuristic - the trigger is simply never reached.
- Rent futures, CDOs, CDS and vice ventures are omitted per the brief as approximately cash-neutral player-to-player transfers.
- In the loan-stimulus variant the $300 is paid by the Treasury and booked onto the player's credit balance; interest flows to the Treasury but repaid principal is modelled as returning to the Bank. This *understates* the Treasury's closing position, so the solvency conclusions under that variant are conservative.
- Draft: snake order, best-available-by-face-value with noise, 8-12 contested deeds at a 25-60% premium, and a player will not commit more than 70% of budget (they lose contests instead).
